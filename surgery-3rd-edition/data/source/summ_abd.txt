Acute Appendicitis
Clinical Definition
• Sudden onset of abdominal pain requiring urgent intervention; • Localized purulent collection in peritoneal compartments to localized right lower quadrant (RLQ) discomfort
• Inflammatory process of the vermiform appendix; • Transition from diffuse visceral (periumbilical) pain to focused RLQ tenderness
Causes
• Intra”abdominal events: - Perforated viscus (peptic ulcer, appendicitis, diverticulitis), - Inflammatory conditions (cholecystitis, pancreatitis); • Trauma/postoperative infections leading to abscess formation; • Extra”abdominal or functional pain etiologies
• Primarily due to luminal obstruction from: - Fecolith, lymphoid hyperplasia, vegetable matter, parasites, or rarely neoplasm; • Progressive ischemia and bacterial invasion
Risk Factors
• Extremes of age (children and elderly); • Immunocompromised status and chronic illnesses (e.g., diabetes); • Previous abdominal surgeries (adhesions, altered anatomy); • Female reproductive age (consider gynecologic emergencies); • Obesity causing delayed or obscured findings
• Extremes of age; • Immunosuppression and metabolic disorders (diabetes); • Variations in appendix position (retrocecal, pelvic); • Prior abdominal surgery impacting local tissue planes
Clinical Features (Symptoms/Signs)
• Diffuse or midline abdominal pain that may later localize; • Nausea and vomiting (often pain precedes vomiting); • Fever ranging from low grade to high; signs of systemic toxicity; • Abdominal distension, rebound tenderness, guarding, tachycardia and hypotension as disease progresses
• Initial anorexia with periumbilical discomfort; • Migratory pain from periumbilical area to RLQ; • Mild vomiting and low”grade fever; • Localized tenderness (McBurney’s point), rebound, guarding; • Special signs (e.g., Rovsing’s, psoas, obturator)
Management (Investigations & Treatments)
• Laboratory tests: CBC (noting leukocytosis), CRP, electrolytes, LFTs, ABG; • Imaging: - Plain X-Ray (detect free air or obstruction signs), - Ultrasound (identify fluid collections, abscesses, cholecystitis), - CT scan for precise localization, abscess detection, wall thickening; • Treatments: - Initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics (covering Gram negatives, anaerobes), - Image–guided percutaneous drainage for localized abscesses, - Urgent surgical intervention when indicated (perforation, ischemia)
• Laboratory studies: CBC (notable neutrophilia), urinalysis, pregnancy test; • Imaging: Ultrasound (enlarged, noncompressible appendix; appendicolith), CT scan to confirm diagnosis if ultrasound is equivocal; • Treatments: - Appendectomy (laparoscopic or open), - Conservative management (for well-localized inflammatory masses, with IV fluids and antibiotics)
Differential Diagnosis & How to Exclude Other Similar DDX
• Consider conditions such as: - Appendicitis, diverticulitis, pancreatitis, cholecystitis; - Gynecological emergencies (ectopic pregnancy, ovarian cysts); - Gastrointestinal perforations; • Exclusion Strategy: Use CT imaging and ultrasound to localize the inflammatory focus, evaluate laboratory inflammatory markers, exclude extra–abdominal causes by correlating imaging findings
• Consider conditions including: - Mesenteric lymphadenitis; - Gastroenteritis, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), urinary tract infection/ureteric stone, ovarian torsion; • Exclusion Strategy: Utilize imaging modalities (US, CT) and pelvic ultrasonography, along with laboratory tests
Complications
• Generalized peritonitis from a perforated viscus; • Sepsis and septic shock; • Intra–abdominal abscess requiring further drainage; • Bowel ischemia/necrosis and subsequent adhesive small bowel obstruction; • Postoperative wound infections, incisional hernia, fistula formation
• Perforation leading to generalized peritonitis; • Formation of a periappendiceal abscess; • Septic shock and systemic inflammatory response; • Postoperative complications (wound infection, ileus, adhesions)
Summary & Key Points
• Rapid, objective diagnostic testing (laboratory and imaging) is essential to distinguish these conditions; • Imaging (X‑Ray, ultrasound, CT) is pivotal both for diagnosis and to exclude alternative DDX; • Treatment is tailored based on findings—urgent surgery is warranted in complicated cases while a conservative regimen may be considered for localized inflammatory masses; • Recognizing specific risk factors and distinct clinical features aids in timely diagnosis and mitigation of complications

Clinical Definition
