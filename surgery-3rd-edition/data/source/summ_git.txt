Minor Salivary Gland Disorders
Tumors of Minor Salivary Glands
Neoplastic growths arising from minor gland tissue; majority are malignant.
Idiopathic, with possible genetic predispositions.
Advanced age; occasional prior radiation exposure.
Benign: slow, painless, mobile mass. Malignant: firm mass, possible ulceration, local invasion.
Excisional or incisional biopsy based on size; imaging (CT/MRI) for lesion delineation; benign: local excision with clear margins; malignant: wide surgical excision (often with reconstruction).
Persistence and absence of acute inflammation distinguish from cystic lesions; histopathology confirms neoplastic character.

Mucous retention within the duct system; a plunging ranula extends through the mylohyoid into the neck.
Ductal disruption from trauma leading to mucus accumulation.
Prior trauma; inherent anatomical predisposition to mucous pooling.
Soft, fluctuant swelling in the floor of the mouth; dumbbell‑shaped if extending into the neck.

Sublingual Salivary Gland Disorders
Tumors of the Sublingual Gland
Neoplastic lesions within the sublingual gland, with most being malignant.
Primarily idiopathic; exact pathogenetic mechanisms remain unclear.
Typically older patients; potential correlation with previous risk exposures.
Rapidly enlarging mass in the floor of the mouth with possible pain and invasion of surrounding tissues.
CT/MRI for assessing extent; fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) for preliminary diagnosis; wide surgical excision with possible neck dissection.
Distinguishable from benign cysts by irregular borders, rapid growth on imaging and cytological features.
High risk of local invasion, metastasis, and recurrence if resection is incomplete.

Bacterial infections often secondary to ductal obstruction; may follow a viral illness.
Dehydration; factors predisposing to duct obstruction.

Longstanding inflammation, often due to recurrent duct obstruction from salivary stones.

Submandibular Salivary Gland Disorders
Tumors of the Submandibular Gland
Neoplastic lesions in the submandibular gland, with roughly half being malignant.
Primarily idiopathic; spontaneous neoplastic transformation is implicated.
Older age; potential history of radiation exposure.
Painless, slow-growing mass – benign tumors are soft and well-circumscribed; malignant lesions are firmer and may invade adjacent tissue.
CT/MRI to outline extent; FNAC for cytological assessment; surgical excision ranging from intracapsular removal (small lesions) to complete gland excision with neck dissection (for malignant tumors).
Imaging and FNAC help exclude reactive adenopathy and inflammatory causes based on growth rate and lesion consistency.

Parotid Gland Disorders
Inflammatory Conditions
Acute inflammation of the parotid gland, which may be viral (e.g., mumps), bacterial, or obstructive in nature.
Viral pathogens (mumps), bacterial infections secondary to obstructive processes, or ductal stones.
Exposure to viral pathogens; dehydration; conditions predisposing to duct obstruction.
Unilateral or bilateral parotid swelling; pain more common in bacterial infection; exacerbation during meals in obstructive cases.
Ultrasonography and sialography revealing gland enlargement and duct dilatation; antibiotic therapy and supportive management for bacterial cases; supportive care for viral infections.
Rapid improvement with conservative management and typical imaging patterns distinguish these from neoplastic masses.
Abscess formation; persistent gland dysfunction if inflammation is unresolved.
Parotid Gland Disorders
Neoplastic Tumors
Tumors of the parotid gland, ranging from benign (most commonly pleomorphic adenoma) to various malignant types.
Mostly idiopathic; some association with prior ionizing radiation exposure.
Advanced age; certain environmental exposures.
Benign: slow, painless, smoothly contoured mass; malignant: may exhibit rapid growth, pain, and possible nodal involvement.
CT/MRI to determine tumor extent and relation to adjacent structures; FNAC to assess cytological features; benign lesions: superficial parotidectomy with careful preservation of facial nerve branches; malignant lesions: radical parotidectomy with neck dissection.
Growth rate, imaging characteristics (irregular margins/invasion), and FNAC findings allow differentiation from inflammatory or benign conditions.
Facial nerve injury, tumor recurrence, and risk of regional metastasis in malignant disease.
Tumor-like Lesions and Systemic Conditions
Sialadenosis
Diffuse, bilateral, non-inflammatory enlargement of salivary glands related to systemic/metabolic disturbances.
Metabolic or endocrine imbalances, including diabetes, chronic alcoholism, pregnancy, and bulimia.
Presence of an underlying systemic disorder affecting gland metabolism.
Painless, symmetric gland enlargement without focal masses or significant tenderness.
Ultrasonography demonstrating diffuse gland enlargement; laboratory evaluations to identify systemic disorders; management focuses on treating the underlying metabolic/endocrine condition.
Lack of focal mass on imaging and correlation with systemic workup excludes neoplastic or localized inflammatory causes.
Persistent cosmetic or functional issues if the primary metabolic disturbance remains uncorrected.
Tumor-like Lesions and Systemic Conditions
Sjögren Syndrome
Autoimmune-mediated progressive lymphocytic destruction of salivary and lacrimal glands.
Autoimmune response affecting glandular tissue; part of a broader spectrum of autoimmune disorders.
More common in middle-aged women; association with other autoimmune diseases.
Marked dryness of the mouth (xerostomia), possible gland enlargement, oral mucosal atrophy, increased susceptibility to candidiasis and dental caries.
Sialography, scintigraphy, and sialochemistry to document gland dysfunction; labial/minor gland biopsy revealing lymphocytic infiltration; serologic markers (autoantibodies) support the diagnosis; systemic immunomodulatory therapy (corticosteroids, cytotoxic agents) and supportive measures.
Diffuse gland involvement with characteristic serologic and biopsy findings distinguishes Sjögren syndrome from localized neoplasms.
Increased risk of lymphoma development; chronic dental and mucosal complications related to persistent xerostomia.

Differential Diagnosis & How to Exclude Other Similar Cases
Differential: IBD-related ulcerations, traumatic anal tears, or anal carcinoma (especially if non-midline); Exclusion: typical posterior midline location with chronic course supports fissure diagnosis, while atypical features warrant biopsy and advanced imaging
Differential: anal fissure, colorectal polyps/carcinoma, or anorectal abscess; Exclusion: endoscopic and imaging evaluations differentiate vascular cushions from mucosal lesions
Differential: perianal hematoma, pilonidal abscess, or traumatic soft tissue infection; Exclusion: imaging studies distinguish a fluid collection (abscess) from a hematoma, coupled with rapid onset and characteristic location
Differential: recurrent anorectal abscess, hidradenitis suppurativa, or infected pilonidal sinus; Exclusion: detailed imaging (ultrasound/MRI) to trace the tract, with characteristic perianal distribution and chronic purulent discharge guiding diagnosis
Differential: infected epidermal inclusion cyst, hidradenitis suppurativa, or perianal abscess; Exclusion: classic midline location in the natal cleft with embedded hair and lateral tracts distinguishes it
Differential: intussusception (especially in children) or mucosal prolapse due to advanced hemorrhoids; Exclusion: dynamic imaging to assess full-thickness versus isolated mucosal involvement and anatomical evaluation to confirm tissue layers
Complications
Postoperative complications such as incontinence (especially flatus) and infection; recurrence if underlying factors (constipation, dietary habits) remain unaddressed
Early complications: pain, urinary retention, or reactionary hemorrhage; Late complications: secondary hemorrhage, local infection, anal stenosis, and potential incontinence if sphincter is compromised (especially post-surgery)
