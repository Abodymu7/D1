1
Goitre
Enlargement of thyroid tissue resulting in an anterior neck mass
Iodine deficiency and endemic goitre; Autoimmune thyroid disorders (e.g. Grave’s disease, nontoxic goitre); Genetic predisposition in endemic areas
Diffuse or nodular swelling in the anterior neck; May present with compressive symptoms and visible pulsation
Thyroid function tests (TFTs show abnormal hormone levels); Ultrasound assesses size, composition, calcifications and vascularity; FNAC when neoplastic features are suspected
Medical management with hormone modulation; Surgical resection when indicated
Exclude thyroid neoplasm using targeted imaging and cytology; Rule out thyroiditis by correlating laboratory markers
Risk of postoperative hypothyroidism; Nerve injury (recurrent laryngeal nerve) during surgery
2
Thyroglossal Cyst/Sinus
Congenital cyst arising from a persistent thyroglossal duct
Failure of the duct to obliterate during thyroid embryologic descent; Typically seen in children (peak incidence ~5 years)
Midline cystic mass near or below the hyoid bone; Moves upward during swallowing and tongue protrusion; May develop a sinus tract postinfection
Ultrasound reveals a well”defined cystic lesion adjacent to the hyoid; CT/MRI and sinogram when fistulous connection is suspected; Thyroid function evaluation to exclude ectopic thyroid tissue
Sistrunk’s procedure (complete excision including a central portion of the hyoid bone)
Exclude ectopic thyroid tissue with functional imaging and hormone studies; Distinguish from midline dermoid cyst by location and mobility characteristics
Recurrence if excision is incomplete; Infection if not managed promptly
3
Submental/Pretracheal Lymphadenopathy
Enlargement of midline lymph nodes in the submental or pretracheal region
Infectious/inflammatory conditions; Possible neoplastic involvement in older adults
May be tender if reactive or firm and non”tender if neoplastic; Often associated with systemic signs in infection (fever, malaise)
Ultrasound to assess node morphology; FNAC for cytologic evaluation differentiating reactive from malignant nodes
Initial antibiotic therapy for suspected infection; Excisional biopsy or further imaging if nodes persist
Exclude malignancy with FNAC and, if needed, core biopsy; Use imaging to differentiate from cystic lesions (e.g. thyroglossal cyst)
Progression to abscess formation if infection is untreated
4
Dermoid Cyst
Benign congenital cyst composed of ectodermal elements
Embryologic sequestration of skin and adnexal structures during development
Painless, soft, and mobile midline mass present since birth; Typically noninflammatory unless secondary infection occurs
Ultrasound shows a cystic lesion with internal echoes; CT scan for precise anatomical delineation if required
Complete surgical excision for cosmetic or symptomatic relief
Differentiate from thyroglossal cyst by lack of movement with swallowing; Exclude lipoma by its firm consistency on imaging
Low risk besides possible infection or recurrence if removal is incomplete
5
Subhyoid Bursitis
Inflammation of the subhyoid bursa located beneath the hyoid bone
Overuse or repetitive microtrauma from neck or submandibular movement
Localized pain and swelling in the subhyoid region; Tenderness on palpation without discrete mass formation
Ultrasound may reveal a well”defined fluid collection with inflammatory signals
Conservative management with NSAIDs and rest; Surgical drainage reserved for refractory or large collections
Exclude abscess formation using imaging characteristics (lack of systemic infection signs); Rule out cystic lesions by the presence of inflammatory fluid rather than a true cyst wall
Recurrence if underlying overuse is not addressed; Infection if the fluid collection becomes superinfected
6
Ranula
Mucous extravasation cyst arising from a disrupted sublingual gland duct
Trauma or idiopathic disruption of the salivary duct
Bluish, transilluminating, cystic swelling on the floor of the mouth; Typically located lateral to the midline
Ultrasound identifies a cystic lesion in the sublingual region; MRI for complex or recurrent cases
Excision or marsupialization combined with identification/treatment of the affected gland
Exclude thyroglossal cyst by noting lack of upward movement with tongue protrusion; Use imaging to distinguish from cystic hygroma based on location and extent
Recurrence if the source gland is not adequately addressed; Infection post”procedure
7
Sebaceous Cyst
Benign epidermal inclusion cyst arising from sebaceous glands
Blockage of sebaceous duct; Minor trauma may contribute; More common in adult populations
Small, dome”shaped, mobile nodule that may become inflamed if secondarily infected
Ultrasound shows a well”circumscribed, cystic lesion with possible internal debris
Simple surgical excision when indicated, particularly for recurrent inflammation
Exclude inflamed abscess by noting absence of systemic signs; Differentiate from lipoma by firmness and imaging characteristics
Recurrence with incomplete excision; Occasional infection requiring antibiotic treatment
8
Lipoma
Benign tumor of adipose tissue presenting as a soft neck mass
Idiopathic; Occasional familial tendency
Soft, mobile, and painless mass with slow growth
Ultrasound reveals a hyperechoic, homogeneous lesion; MRI for further tissue characterization if indicated
Surgical excision for symptomatic relief or cosmetic reasons
Differentiate from cystic lesions by homogeneous, fat”like appearance on imaging; Rule out other soft tissue tumors with imaging and, if needed, biopsy
Minimal if completely removed; Low risk of recurrence
9
Cervical Lymphadenopathy
Enlargement of cervical lymph nodes (reactive or neoplastic)
Acute infections (viral, bacterial) and chronic infections (TB, toxoplasmosis); Lymphomas (Hodgkin’s and non”Hodgkin’s) and metastatic carcinoma from head/neck primaries; Autoimmune conditions (e.g. Kikuchi disease)
Unilateral or multiple nodal enlargements with possible tenderness; Firm and fixed nodes in malignant processes; May be accompanied by systemic signs
Blood tests (ESR, specific serological markers); Ultrasound to assess nodal architecture (loss of hilum in malignancy); FNAC for cytologic evaluation; CXR and targeted fiberoptic imaging
Empirical antibiotic therapy for suspected infectious etiologies; Excisional biopsy if persistence or malignancy is suspected
Exclude lymphoma with repeat FNAC/core biopsy and imaging; Distinguish from metastatic nodes by assessing primary head/neck lesions
Abscess formation if untreated; Systemic spread in case of lymphoma or metastatic disease
10
Branchial Cleft Anomalies (Cyst/Fistula)
Congenital malformation from failure of branchial cleft obliteration
Most frequently involves the second branchial cleft; Less commonly 1st, 3rd, or 4th branchial cleft
Presents as a lateral cyst, sinus, or fistula usually along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle; May leak fluid or become secondarily infected
Ultrasound and CT/MRI to delineate the tract and surrounding vascular relationships; Sinogram/fistulogram to outline fistulous connection
Complete surgical excision via a curvilinear or anterior SCM incision while preserving neurovascular structures
Exclude lymphadenopathy by demonstrating a defined sinus tract on sinography; Rule out paraganglioma with contrast”enhanced imaging
Recurrence if tract is incompletely removed; Risk of injury to carotid artery, internal jugular vein, or cranial nerves
11
Cystic Hygroma
Congenital lymphatic malformation presenting as a cystic mass, most commonly in the posterior triangle
Developmental anomaly of lymphatic channels, typically presenting in early childhood (<2 years)
Soft, compressible, and fluctuant mass that often transilluminates; May be seen in areas beyond the posterior triangle (face, axilla, mediastinum)
Ultrasound shows multicystic, fluid”filled spaces; CT with contrast or MRI to assess extent and relation to vital structures
Surgical resection if complete removal is feasible; Sclerotherapy (OK”432, bleomycin, doxycycline) for lesions involving critical structures
Differentiate from abscess or cystic metastasis using fluid characteristics and clinical context; Confirm lymphatic nature with multimodal imaging
Recurrence if complete resection is not achievable; Potential injury to adjacent neurovascular structures
12
Chemoductoma (Carotid Body Tumor)
Rare, typically benign neoplasm of the carotid body often termed ‘potato tumour’
Often occurs in older patients; Degenerative changes may play a role
Firm lateral neck mass located at the carotid bifurcation; Mobile side”to”side yet fixed vertically; Usually non”tender
Ultrasound and CT scan with contrast show a solid, enhancing mass near the carotid bifurcation; Elastography helps differentiate from lymph nodes
Surgical excision with careful dissection and possible arterial shunting
Exclude metastatic lymphadenopathy by noting characteristic vascular enhancement; Differentiate from other paragangliomas with precise anatomical localization
Risk of injury to adjacent carotid artery, jugular vein, or cranial nerves (hypoglossal, marginal mandibular)
13
Cervical Rib
An anatomical variant defined by an extra rib arising from a cervical vertebra
Congenital anomaly; May be sporadic or familial
Usually asymptomatic and discovered incidentally; When palpable, presents as a hard mass in the posterior triangle; May be associated with thoracic outlet syndrome symptoms
Plain radiograph (cervical X”ray) confirms bony structure; CT scan for detailed anatomical evaluation if symptoms are present
Conservative management for asymptomatic cases; Surgical resection for symptomatic thoracic outlet syndrome
Distinguish from soft tissue masses with radiographic evidence of bone; Rule out neoplastic processes with imaging correlation
Neurovascular compression resulting in pain, paresthesia, or vascular compromise if left untreated
14
Diseases of Function (Hyperthyroidism & Hypothyroidism)
Disorders primarily affecting thyroid hormone production without significant gland enlargement
Hyperthyroidism: immune-mediated (Grave’s disease), toxic adenomas, post-treatment iatrogenic causes; Hypothyroidism: thyroiditis, post-ablative changes, iodine deficiency
Hyperthyroidism: tremor, palpitations, sweating, weight loss; Hypothyroidism: lethargy, weight gain, cold intolerance
TFTs (TSH, T3, T4) show hormone imbalances; Ultrasound to evaluate gland size and detect nodules
Hyperthyroidism: beta blockers and antithyroid agents; Hypothyroidism: lifelong thyroxine replacement with dose titration based on TSH levels
Exclude destructive thyroiditis by serial hormone testing and inflammatory markers; Use imaging to rule out nodular causes when gland function is normal
Cardiac arrhythmias in hyperthyroidism; Myxedema and metabolic slowdown in hypothyroidism
15
Disorders of Size (Simple Goitre, nSTN, nMNG)
Thyroid enlargement with or without functional abnormality, presenting as solitary or multinodular goitre
Nutritional iodine deficiency; Genetic predisposition and locally autonomous nodular disease
Visible neck swelling; May produce compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnea, stridor); Cosmetic concerns
Ultrasound differentiates solid versus cystic components, calcification, and elastography characteristics; FNAC for cytological evaluation; Isotope scanning for functional mapping
Thyroid suppression therapy (thyroxine) for benign cases; Hemithyroidectomy or total thyroidectomy for suspicious features
Distinguish from thyroid carcinoma via FNAC and targeted imaging; Exclude thyroiditis by ultrasound texture and antibody markers
Compressive symptoms and risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury during surgery
16
Grave’s Disease
Diffuse toxic goitre with autoimmune hyperfunction often accompanied by ophthalmopathy
Autoimmune etiology; Frequently in young to middle-aged patients
Features of hyperthyroidism with diffuse, vascular gland enlargement; May have ocular involvement (exophthalmos)
TFTs confirm hyperthyroidism; Ultrasound shows a hypervascular gland; Isotope scan if needed
Medical stabilization with beta blockers and antithyroid medications; Radioiodine therapy or near-total/total thyroidectomy based on patient factors
Exclude toxic multinodular goitre via imaging; Confirm diffuse gland involvement
Risk of thyroid storm if inadequately controlled; Post-therapeutic hypothyroidism and recurrent ophthalmopathy
17
Recurrent Goitre
Regrowth of thyroid tissue after previous surgery or treatment
Previous thyroid surgery with persistent or regrown tissue due to surgical scarring; Increased operative risks from distorted anatomy
Re-emergence of neck swelling possibly with compressive symptoms; Higher risk profile for nerve injury
Repeat ultrasound and imaging to assess anatomical distortion and lymph node involvement
Consider thyroid suppression or radioiodine therapy; Reoperation reserved for severe symptomatic cases
Exclude residual disease with imaging and selective FNAC sampling
Increased risk of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and hypocalcemia due to scar tissue
18
Primary HPT
Excess parathyroid hormone secretion from one or more parathyroid glands causing hypercalcemia
Most commonly a solitary adenoma; Parathyroid hyperplasia or carcinoma less frequent; Genetic predispositions may play a role
Symptoms related to hypercalcemia (‘stones, bones, groans, and psychiatric overtones’); May be asymptomatic in mild cases
Elevated serum PTH and calcium, low phosphate, increased alkaline phosphatase; Localization studies with ultrasound, CT, MRI or Sestamibi scan
Surgical excision (adenoma removal); Subtotal parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation in hyperplasia; Total parathyroidectomy in carcinoma with possible thyroid and lymph node dissection
Differentiated from secondary HPT by biochemical profile; Localization studies help distinguish solitary adenoma from multiglandular disease
Postoperative hypocalcemia (hungry bone syndrome); Recurrent or persistent hyperparathyroidism if resection is incomplete
19
Secondary HPT
Parathyroid hyperplasia as a compensatory response to chronic hypocalcemia, often seen in chronic renal failure
Chronic renal failure; Prolonged vitamin D deficiency; Certain drug exposures
Mildly elevated calcium with high PTH; Bone pain, soft tissue calcifications, renal osteodystrophy
High PTH, low/normal calcium, high phosphate; Impaired renal function on laboratory tests
Correction of the underlying cause; Surgical intervention (subtotal or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation) for symptomatic cases
Exclude primary HPT by correlating with renal function and biochemical markers
Progressive bone disease and vascular calcifications if untreated
20
Tertiary HPT
Autonomous parathyroid hyperfunction following long-term secondary stimulation
Longstanding renal failure after correction of the primary cause
Persistent hypercalcemia despite treatment for secondary causes; Worsening bone disease
Biochemical profile similar to primary HPT with history of chronic renal failure; Localization studies for surgical planning
Surgical intervention (subtotal or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation), often delayed until post-transplant stability
Differentiate from secondary HPT by persistence of hypercalcemia after primary correction
Severe hypercalcemia with bone demineralization and renal calcifications
